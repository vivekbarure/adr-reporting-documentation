# Causality Assessment

## Pharmacovigilance and Drug Safety --- Educational Project

**Task:** Causality Assessment (Task 3)\
**Purpose:** To evaluate the relationship between a suspected medicine
and an adverse reaction using standard causality assessment principles.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## 1. Introduction

Causality assessment is the structured evaluation of whether a medicine
may have caused a suspected adverse drug reaction (ADR). It considers
the timing of the event, the known safety profile of the medicine,
alternative explanations, response to stopping or restarting the
medicine, and available clinical evidence.

A causality category is an assessment, not absolute proof. The method
used and the information available can affect the result.

## 2. Objectives

-   Understand the purpose of causality assessment.
-   Identify the information needed to evaluate a suspected ADR.
-   Understand the categories Certain, Probable/Likely, and Possible.
-   Apply the principles to educational case studies.
-   Document conclusions transparently and avoid overstating certainty.

## 3. Key factors in causality assessment

1.  **Time relationship:** Did the reaction begin after the medicine was
    started, and is the timing biologically plausible?
2.  **Known reaction:** Is the reaction described in reliable medicine
    safety information?
3.  **Dechallenge:** Did the reaction improve after the medicine was
    reduced or stopped? Improvement may also have other explanations.
4.  **Rechallenge:** Did the reaction recur after the medicine was
    restarted? Rechallenge is not usually performed intentionally when
    it could be unsafe.
5.  **Alternative causes:** Could the underlying disease, another
    medicine, an infection, or another condition explain the event?
6.  **Objective evidence:** Are there examination findings, laboratory
    results, or other clinical evidence supporting the assessment?
7.  **Data quality:** Are the medicine, dose, dates, symptoms, and
    outcome recorded clearly?

## 4. Common causality categories

Terminology varies by assessment system. The following are simplified
educational descriptions.

-   **Certain:** A clear and plausible time relationship exists; other
    causes are unlikely; the event fits the medicine's known effects or
    has convincing evidence; and the clinical course supports the
    relationship. Rechallenge evidence may help but is not always
    required or appropriate.
-   **Probable/Likely:** The timing is reasonable, other explanations
    are unlikely, and improvement after withdrawal supports a
    medicine-related cause. Rechallenge is generally not necessary.
-   **Possible:** The timing is reasonable, but the event could also be
    explained by the disease or another medicine, or withdrawal
    information is unclear.
-   **Unlikely:** The timing or clinical evidence makes a
    medicine-related cause improbable.
-   **Conditional/Unclassified or Unassessable/Unclassifiable:** More
    information is needed, or the information is insufficient or
    contradictory.

This project focuses on Certain, Probable, and Possible, as requested in
the task description. Real assessments should follow the specified local
or institutional method.

## 5. Case study 1 --- Possible

**Fictional scenario:** A patient taking a prescribed medicine develops
nausea two days after starting treatment. The patient also has a stomach
infection, and there is no information about what happened after the
medicine was stopped.

**Assessment: Possible**

**Reasoning:** The timing may fit a medicine-related reaction, but the
infection is an alternative explanation. The outcome after stopping the
medicine is unknown, so a stronger conclusion is not justified.

## 6. Case study 2 --- Probable/Likely

**Fictional scenario:** A patient starts a new medicine and develops
ankle swelling several days later. A healthcare professional reviews the
case, finds the timing and reaction consistent with the medicine's known
effects, and observes that the swelling improves after the prescriber
stops the medicine. No more likely alternative cause is identified.

**Assessment: Probable/Likely**

**Reasoning:** The timing is plausible, the reaction is consistent with
the known safety profile, improvement after withdrawal supports the
association, and no stronger alternative explanation is identified.

## 7. Case study 3 --- Certain (illustrative)

**Fictional scenario:** A patient develops a distinctive, objectively
documented reaction shortly after receiving a medicine. Clinical
assessment finds a highly characteristic pattern, alternative causes are
reasonably excluded, and the available evidence strongly supports the
medicine as the cause.

**Assessment: Certain --- only if the full clinical record meets the
criteria of the assessment system being used.**

**Reasoning:** A Certain classification requires strong, coherent
evidence and careful consideration of alternative explanations. This
fictional summary illustrates the concept; a real case cannot be
classified as Certain without sufficient case-specific evidence.

## 8. Practical assessment checklist

-   Confirm the suspected medicine, dose, route, and dates.
-   Record the reaction and its onset date.
-   Review known adverse effects and relevant medical history.
-   Check other medicines and possible alternative causes.
-   Document any change after withdrawal (dechallenge).
-   Record any previous accidental re-exposure (rechallenge), if
    applicable; do not deliberately re-expose a patient just to test
    causality.
-   Apply the required assessment method consistently.
-   Record the category, rationale, missing information, and
    assessor/date.
-   Report suspected ADRs through the appropriate pharmacovigilance
    system.

## 9. Conclusion

Causality assessment helps healthcare professionals evaluate suspected
links between medicines and adverse reactions. The conclusion should
match the strength of the evidence, consider alternative causes, and
clearly explain uncertainty. A suspected ADR report is important for
safety monitoring, but it does not automatically prove that a medicine
caused the event.
